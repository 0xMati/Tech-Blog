## 0. What this article actually delivers 📋

The previous article explained *why* RC4 is dangerous and *where* it tends to hide. This one explains *how to find every single place it still hides in your environment*, so you can disable it without breaking production.

Think of it like a building inspection before demolition. You don't take a wrecking ball to a wall until you know which ones are load-bearing. Same thing here: you don't push an AES-only policy until you have a ranked, owner-assigned list of every account, device, and trust that would break.

**The deliverable at the end of this process is a single spreadsheet** (or backlog item, or wiki page — same content, different container) that looks like this:

| Dependency | Type | RC4 evidence | Blast radius | Fix cost | Score | Owner | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `svc_sql_legacy` | Service account | 4 769 RC4 tickets / 7 days | High (prod DB) | Low (password rotation + retest) | 9 | DB Team | Rotate secret, re-validate, then drop RC4 |
| `printsrv-bio-01$` | Computer account | 812 RC4 tickets / 7 days | Medium (biomed printer pool) | High (vendor firmware) | 6 | Infra Team | Temporary exception until vendor patch, EOL plan |
| `Trust: corp.contoso → partner.legacy` | TDO | RC4 referrals daily | High (partner B2B) | Medium (coordinate password rotation across forests) | 8 | Identity Team | Rotate trust password, set TDO to AES, validate |

That table is the entire point. Every section below is a step to fill it in.

## 1. Scope and Collection Rules

Before running scripts everywhere, lock the rules.

Minimum scope:

- All domain controllers in scope.
- SPN-bearing identities (service accounts).
- Critical machine accounts and service accounts.
- Trust relationships (TDO) for multi-domain or multi-forest environments.
- Non-Windows systems using Kerberos (if present).

Baseline rules:

- Use a representative observation window (not 15 minutes during low traffic).
- Correlate AD configuration with runtime telemetry.
- **Enable Kerberos audit policies on every DC** — *Audit Kerberos Authentication Service* (events 4768 / 4771) and *Audit Kerberos Service Ticket Operations* (event 4769), both set to **Success** at minimum. Configured under Advanced Audit Policy Configuration → Account Logon. Without these subcategories enabled, the Security log is silent on Kerberos and Source 1 produces nothing — the observation window is open but blind. Verify with `auditpol /get /category:"Account Logon"` on each DC before opening the window.
- **Enable DC-side Kdcsvc audit** — set `RC4DefaultDisablementPhase = 1` on every DC in scope (KB5073381, January 2026). This is what produces the Kdcsvc 201-209 events in the System log used by §2 Source 0. At Phase 0 or with the registry value absent, the KDC stays silent on this channel. Phase 1 is **audit-only** and does not change ticket issuance.
- **Size the Security log accordingly.** A busy DC at full Kerberos audit can write hundreds of MB / day. Default 20 MB will roll over in minutes and silently truncate your window. Raise to at least 1 GB on each DC, or forward to a SIEM, before starting collection.
- Do not change policy before dependencies are qualified — specifically, do **not** move to `RC4DefaultDisablementPhase = 2` (enforce) until the backlog is empty.

Discipline during collection — inventory is **read-only**, no exception:

- Do **not** modify `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` on any account. Even on accounts that look obviously misconfigured.
- Do **not** rotate passwords "to test" before the inventory exists. You will move dependencies into the stale-key trap and lose the runtime evidence you needed.
- Do **not** push an AES-only GPO "in parallel of the audit". This is the exact failure mode described in [Article 1 §5](./1.%20RC4%20Fundamentals%2C%20Obsolescence%2C%20and%20Risks%20of%20Continued%20Use.md).
- Do **not** correlate from a partial dataset (single DC, low-traffic window). Re-read the observation-window rules below.

### Why the observation window matters

Kerberos traffic is not uniform. A 15-minute capture during a quiet afternoon will miss the legacy ETL job that runs nightly, the SCCM client that authenticates only at boot, the monthly billing batch on `svc_billing_eom`, and the partner B2B trust that's only used during business hours in another timezone.

Rule of thumb for the observation window:

- **Minimum 7 calendar days** so every daily/nightly batch is captured at least once.
- **Cover one full business cycle**: ideally include a Patch Tuesday, a weekend, and a month-end if your environment has billing/reporting cycles.
- **Capture from every DC**, not a sample. RC4 routinely hides on one specific DC because of how clients pin to KDCs.
- **Every DC at Phase ≥ 1** during the entire window. A DC promoted, rebuilt, or rolled back to Phase 0 mid-window creates a hole that no amount of post-collection correlation can fill. The companion script reports DCs not at Phase ≥ 1 as a blind-spot KPI; treat any non-zero count as a reason to extend the window after fixing the gap.
- **Don't collect during a freeze window** — traffic shape is artificially distorted and you'll miss real dependencies.

If you must move fast, a 7-day window across all DCs *with Phase ≥ 1 throughout* is the floor. Two weeks is the realistic minimum for a confident inventory.

## 2. Technical Data Sources to Correlate

Never trust a single source.

| Source | What it provides | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source 0a** — `DefaultDomainSupportedEncTypes` (HKLM `...\Kerberos\Parameters`, each DC) | DC-side capability advertised in TGTs (KDC posture) | Implicit AES default since KB5021131 (Nov 2022); explicit value still needed to detect Pattern E hygiene; per-DC, not per-domain |
| **Source 0b** — `RC4DefaultDisablementPhase` (HKLM `...\Kerberos\Parameters`, KB5073381 / Jan 2026) | Per-DC audit/enforce switch and the trigger for Source 0c events | Phase `0` or absent = silent (Source 0c blind); registry valve removed July 2026; per-DC, must be `≥ 1` everywhere during the window |
| **Source 0c** — Kdcsvc System log events 201-209 | Direct KDC-side flagging of RC4 negotiations, offending account already extracted, Pattern (B / D / Hygiene) implicit in the event ID | Requires Phase ≥ 1; events disappear once the registry valve is removed in July 2026 |
| **Source 1a** — Event 4768 (Security log) | TGT issuance and AS-REQ etype context. Post-Jan-2025: `Available Keys`, `Advertized Etypes`, `Pre-Authentication EncryptionType`, `Session Encryption Type` | Requires *Audit Kerberos Authentication Service = Success*; pre-Jan-2025 the `Ticket Encryption Type` field actually meant session key; high volume |
| **Source 1b** — Event 4769 (Security log) | TGS issuance — real service impact, the canonical aggregation feed. Post-Jan-2025: same enriched fields as 4768 | Requires *Audit Kerberos Service Ticket Operations = Success*; high volume; needs aggregation by `ServiceName` × `TicketEncryptionType` |
| **Source 1c** — Event 4771 (Security log) | Kerberos pre-auth failures — surfaces clients refusing AES when the DC pushes for it (cross-check with Pattern B candidates) | Same audit subcategory as 4768; noisier than 4768/4769 for inventory; useful as a confirmation, not a primary feed |
| **Source 2** — `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` (per account, LDAP) | Account-level capability bitmask — *intent* of the account or its provisioning system | Capability ≠ effective key material (see Source 4); absent / zero value ≠ remediated — Microsoft documents it falls back to RC4 |
| **Source 3** — Trust objects (TDO) — `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` + `pwdLastSet` | Cross-domain / cross-forest surface, attribute *and* key-rotation evidence | Default referral behaviour changed with KB5021131 (Nov 2022); attribute alone insufficient — AES keys only materialize after a post-attribute password rotation; **LOCAL side only** — the twin TDO must be audited on the remote forest |
| **Source 4** — Stale-key correlation (cross-cutting) | Combines Source 2/3 capability with `pwdLastSet` vs. AES domain cutoff to flag accounts that advertise AES but still emit RC4 | Not an event feed — a *derivation* over the others; depends on collection quality of all the above |

### What each source actually tells you

For each source above, the key is to know *what question it answers* and *what it cannot tell you on its own*. The collection scripts (KQL, PowerShell, `nltest`) live in the dedicated **RC4 telemetry & inventory reporting** annex — this article focuses on what the data means once it lands in the inventory spreadsheet.

#### Source 0a — `DefaultDomainSupportedEncTypes` (DC-side capability)

Read directly from `HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Services\Kdc\Parameters\DefaultDomainSupportedEncTypes` on each DC (or via `Invoke-Command`). It is a **per-DC** setting, not a domain-wide GPO, so a single non-conformant DC is enough to skew runtime behaviour.

- Implicit default since KB5021131 (Nov 2022) is `0x27` (RC4 + AES128 + AES256 + a compat bit) — safe enough that *absent* registry value is acceptable.
- An explicit value with the RC4 bit set (`0x04` or any combination including it) is what raises Kdcsvc **205** (Pattern Hygiene). Detection does not require Phase ≥ 1.
- The companion script reads it via `Get-KdcDefaultAudit` — independent of Source 0c collection, so this signal is available even on a Phase-0 estate.

#### Source 0b — `RC4DefaultDisablementPhase` (KB5073381, Jan 2026)

Per-DC valve at `HKLM\...\Kerberos\Parameters`. Decodes to:

| Value | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `0` or absent | Silent — Source 0c produces **nothing**. |
| `1` | Audit — events 201/202/205/206/207 emitted as warnings; ticket issuance unchanged. |
| `2` | Enforce — events 203/204/208/209 emitted as errors; the KDC actively rejects. |

Inventory rule: every DC must be at `1` for the entire observation window. The script reports DCs not at Phase ≥ 1 as a blind-spot KPI. The registry valve disappears in July 2026, after which AES-only enforcement is permanent.

#### Source 0c — Kdcsvc events 201-209 (the cheapest evidence)

If Source 0b is at `1` or higher, **start here**. The KDC itself logs each RC4 negotiation it sees, with the offending account name pre-extracted, in the System log under provider `Microsoft-Windows-Kerberos-Key-Distribution-Center`. No SACL to configure, no etype filtering on millions of 4769 records, no LDAP correlation needed for the first cut. The full decoder lives in [Article 1 §6](./1.%20RC4%20Fundamentals%2C%20Obsolescence%2C%20and%20Risks%20of%20Continued%20Use.md).

Quick mapping for inventory purposes:

| Event ID | Pattern | What it identifies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **201 / 206** | B (audit) | A *client* that is RC4-only |
| **202 / 207** | D (audit) | A *service / target account* with no AES key — stale-key trap |
| **205** | Hygiene | DC with explicit insecure `DefaultDomainSupportedEncTypes` (no inventory row, fix on the DC) |
| **203 / 204 / 208 / 209** | B / D (enforce) | Same conditions once Phase 2 is on — errors instead of warnings |

Two caveats: (1) silent at Phase 0; (2) does not exist at all on an unpatched estate, in which case Source 1 is your only runtime feed.

#### Source 1a / 1b — TGT and TGS events (4768 / 4769)

The single most important runtime signal once Kdcsvc is drained — or your only signal if you don't have KB5073381. Filter both on `TicketEncryptionType == 0x17` (decimal 23 = RC4-HMAC). Aggregate **4769 by `ServiceName`** to get the prioritization order — highest-volume RC4 service tickets are the highest-impact remediation targets.

Reference etype values you'll see in the wild:

| Hex | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `0x01` | DES-CBC-CRC |
| `0x03` | DES-CBC-MD5 |
| `0x11` | AES128-CTS-HMAC-SHA1-96 |
| `0x12` | AES256-CTS-HMAC-SHA1-96 |
| `0x17` | RC4-HMAC |
| `0x18` (event field) | **RC4-HMAC-EXP** ⚠️ not AES |

DES etypes (`0x01`, `0x03`) are out of scope for this RC4 inventory but are a priority-0 finding if observed — they should not exist on any modern domain.

> ⚠️ Reminder from Article 1 §6: in the **event** `Ticket Encryption Type` field, `0x18` is **RC4-HMAC-EXP**, not AES. Do not confuse it with the same hex on the `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` **attribute**, where `0x18` means AES128 + AES256. Same number, two different namespaces.

#### Source 1a deep dive — using 4768 to find RC4-only client devices

4768 is often dismissed as *"the AS event, less interesting than 4769"*. That is half wrong. There is one specific question that **only 4768 can answer**: *which client devices are RC4-only on AS-REQ?*

**Mechanism.** When a Kerberos client sends an AS-REQ to obtain a TGT, the KDC selects a session key etype based on what the **client** advertised. On modern domains, the TGT envelope itself is almost always AES (DFL ≥ 2008 + `krbtgt` with AES keys), but the session key etype reflects the client's capability set. Up to and including the January 2025 cumulative update, the `Ticket Encryption Type` field on 4768 reported the **session key** etype, not the envelope etype. Post-Jan-2025, a separate `Session Encryption Type` field reports it directly.

**Application for the inventory:**

- Filter `4768` on RC4 in the relevant field (`Ticket Encryption Type` pre Jan 2025, `Session Encryption Type` post Jan 2025).
- Aggregate by `Client Address` (source IP) and `Account Name`.
- A device that *consistently* appears with RC4 in the AS flow is a Kerberos client that only supports RC4 — your **device-level** RC4 dependency, distinct from the **account-level** dependencies surfaced by 4769.
- Catches: Windows hosts with AES disabled by GPO or registry, legacy XP / 2003 hosts, non-Windows appliances and Linux hosts with `krb5.conf` pinned to RC4, devices using keytabs that only contain RC4 keys.

Methodology documented in Microsoft's *Active Directory Hardening Series – Part 4 – Enforcing AES for Kerberos* (Jerry Devore, MSFT).

**Limitation — passive Kerberos consumers (keytab-only services).** A non-Windows system with a static keytab can be a critical RC4 consumer **without ever appearing in 4768**. The mechanism :

1. An admin runs `ktpass -princ HTTP/appliance01.contoso.com -mapuser svc_appliance -crypto RC4-HMAC-NT -out appliance.keytab` on a Windows DC. This creates / updates the AD account `svc_appliance`, attaches the SPN to it, derives a key from the password, and writes that key into the keytab file.
2. The keytab is copied to `/etc/krb5.keytab` on the appliance.
3. Clients request TGS for `HTTP/appliance01` → the KDC encrypts the ticket with `svc_appliance`'s long-term key → 4769 is logged on the DC.
4. The appliance receives the TGS and decrypts it locally with its keytab. **It never contacts the DC**, so no 4768 / 4769 is generated *for the appliance itself*.

The AD account behind the SPN always exists — that's how the KDC knows which key to use. So `svc_appliance` is **fully visible** through Source 1b (4769 with `ServiceName = svc_appliance`), Source 2 (its `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes`) and Source 4 (its `pwdLastSet`). What's missing from 4768 alone is the **device-level mapping** — which physical / virtual host carries the keytab. Detection requires a complementary field inventory: `klist -k /etc/krb5.keytab` on each non-Windows host, or a CMDB attribute that ties SPNs to hosts.

> 🚨 **Closely related trap — `lastLogonTimestamp` does not reflect SPN consumption.** The attribute is updated only when the account *itself* authenticates (interactive logon, network logon, or `kinit`). When the KDC merely uses the account's long-term key to encrypt a TGS for the SPN, **`lastLogonTimestamp` is not bumped**. Consequences:
>
> - A passive service account (Linux / Unix appliance keytab, SQL Server cluster SPN holder, certain RBCD targets, gMSA in some hybrid topologies) can show `lastLogonTimestamp = 0` or a value years old while clients consume thousands of TGS for it daily.
> - Standard "disable accounts inactive > N days" cleanup scripts will **disable the SPN-bearing account**, killing every dependent service at once. Worse, a script that resets the password or deletes the account invalidates the long-term key — every existing keytab becomes useless until regenerated and redeployed.
> - **For the RC4 inventory, never use `lastLogonTimestamp` as a "this account is unused" filter.** Use **Source 1b (4769) by `ServiceName`** as the authoritative usage signal. Any account producing TGS during the observation window is in use, regardless of its logon attributes.
>
> This is the operational reason behind the §1 *Scope* rule "include accounts that look inactive": the most dangerous accounts to break are precisely the ones that look dead.

#### Source 1a / 1b deep dive — January 2025 enriched fields

If your DCs are at or above the January 2025 cumulative update (Server 2016+), 4768 and 4769 expose new fields that shortcut several steps of this article.

| Field | What it tells you | Why it shortcuts inventory work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `MSDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` (account / service) | Per-account capability bitmask, inside the event | No need to LDAP-query each account separately when triaging |
| `Available Keys` | Which etypes the account *actually* has key material for | **Direct evidence of the stale-key trap** — AES-SHA1 absent on an account advertising AES = confirmed without correlating `pwdLastSet` |
| `Advertized Etypes` | Etypes the client offered in the AS-REQ / TGS-REQ | Confirms whether the client is really RC4-only or whether the choice is service-driven |
| `Pre-Authentication EncryptionType` | Etype of the pre-auth blob | Should match the strongest entry in `Advertized Etypes`; mismatches indicate misconfigured clients |
| `Session Encryption Type` | Session key etype, decoupled from envelope etype | Removes the pre-Jan-2025 ambiguity where `Ticket Encryption Type` on 4768 actually meant session key |

The **RC4 telemetry & inventory reporting** annex (to be created) will provide queries that branch on whether these fields are present, so the same inventory works on pre- and post-Jan-2025 telemetry.

#### Source 1c — Event 4771 (pre-auth failures)

Confirmation feed, not a primary one. A client flagged as Pattern B by Kdcsvc 201/206 will typically also surface here when it refuses an AES pre-auth challenge. Useful to:

- Cross-check Source 0c findings on accounts you cannot easily attribute by IP alone.
- Detect Pattern B candidates on an estate that is not yet at Phase ≥ 1 (when Source 0c is silent).

Same audit subcategory as 4768 (*Audit Kerberos Authentication Service*), so no extra collection cost once 4768 is on.

#### Source 2 — `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` (account-level capability)

Tells you which accounts **advertise** RC4. Use the LDAP bitwise-OR matching rule (OID `1.2.840.113556.1.4.804`) to filter on the `0x04` bit.

Two caveats:

1. **An absent or zero value still counts as an RC4 producer.** Since KB5021131 the KDC interprets the implicit default as AES + RC4 (it does not silently upgrade to AES-only), so accounts with no explicit value must be counted as RC4 emitters until proven otherwise via runtime evidence.
2. **Capability ≠ actual key material.** An account can carry the AES bits but still emit RC4 because the password predates AES support — see Source 4.

#### Source 3 — Trust posture (TDOs)

`Get-ADTrust` exposes `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` and trust direction. Cross-check with the trust password age — `pwdLastSet` on the TDO object, or `nltest /domain_trusts /v` for human-readable output.

Important nuance: since KB5021131 (Nov 2022 / CVE-2022-37966), Microsoft DCs default referral tickets to AES regardless of the TDO `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` value — **provided the trust password contains AES keys**. Two consequences:

- A trust set to `0x18` (AES-only) **with an old password** is more dangerous than a trust set to `0x00`: it advertises AES but emits RC4 anyway. This is the trust flavour of the stale-key trap.
- The audit must be run on **both sides** of the trust. The companion script's `Invoke-TrustEncryptionAudit` covers only the local TDO — the twin must be audited on the partner forest.

Article 3 §3.3 covers the rotation discipline.

#### Source 4 — Stale keys (cross-cutting derivation)

An account can advertise AES capability and still emit RC4 because its password predates AES support and was never rotated. Not a feed in itself — a **correlation** over Sources 1, 2, 3.

Signals to combine:

- `PasswordLastSet` per account (Source 2).
- `whenChanged` / `pwdLastSet` on TDOs (Source 3).
- `PasswordLastSet` on `krbtgt` (covers the Kerberos service master key).
- `0x18` capability on `msDS-SET` with an old password = stale-key candidate.

> 🔍 **Microsoft pro tip for the cutoff date.** AES support became available in your domain when the first DC at functional level 2008 was promoted — exactly the moment the built-in group `Read-only Domain Controllers` was created. Comparing each account's `PasswordLastSet` to `(Get-ADGroup 'Read-only Domain Controllers').whenCreated` gives you the stale-key candidate list without arbitrary date heuristics.

The remediation — password rotation — lives in Article 3.

## 3. Recommended Inventory Pipeline

```mermaid
flowchart LR
	A[Collect 4768/4769] --> B[Extract RC4 and AES usage]
	B --> C[Join with AD objects]
	C --> D[Identify dependencies]
	D --> E[Score risk and priority]
	E --> F[Build remediation backlog]
```

Practical sequence:

1. Collect 4768/4769 over a representative window.
2. Isolate RC4 tickets and aggregate by target service (SPN/account).
3. Join with AD attributes (`msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes`, account type, service criticality).
4. Verify whether AES keys are actually present for these identities.
5. Produce a prioritized dependency list with proposed actions.

### Worked example: from raw events to backlog

Make it concrete. Assume a mid-size domain: 4 DCs, 500 servers, 12 service accounts known to ops, 3 cross-forest trusts. You run the collection over 14 days.

**After step 1-2 (KQL aggregated by SPN, RC4 only)**:

| ServiceName | RC4 ticket count (14 d) | Distinct clients |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `MSSQLSvc/sql01.corp.contoso.com:1433` | 12 847 | 38 |
| `HTTP/intranet.corp.contoso.com` | 4 210 | 412 |
| `krbtgt/PARTNER.LEGACY` | 893 | 6 |
| `host/printsrv-bio-01.corp.contoso.com` | 812 | 23 |
| `cifs/fileserver-old.corp.contoso.com` | 142 | 9 |

**After step 3 (joined with AD)**:

| ServiceName | Account | `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` | PasswordLastSet |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `MSSQLSvc/sql01…` | `svc_sql_legacy` | `28` (`0x1C`, AES+RC4) | 2021-03-04 |
| `HTTP/intranet…` | `svc_iis_intranet` | `28` (`0x1C`, AES+RC4) | 2026-04-12 |
| `krbtgt/PARTNER.LEGACY` | Trust (TDO) | `0` (default = RC4 allowed) | 2019-11-22 |
| `host/printsrv-bio-01` | `printsrv-bio-01$` | `4` (`0x04`, RC4-only) | 2023-08-15 |
| `cifs/fileserver-old` | `fileserver-old$` | `28` (`0x1C`) | 2025-09-30 |

Already useful: `svc_iis_intranet` supports AES (`0x1C`) **and** has a recent password rotation, so the AES key is freshly derived and present in AD — yet RC4 tickets are still being issued for it. That rules out Pattern D (stale-key trap, see §5) and points at the client side or the negotiation path: clients still requesting RC4 in their TGS-REQ etype list, or a downstream consumer that can only handle RC4. Worth investigating before touching anything else: this is exactly the kind of case where the post-January-2025 `Advertised Etypes` field on 4769 settles the question in seconds — it shows what the *client* offered, separating a server-side stale-key issue from a client-side legacy stack.

**After step 4-5 (qualified backlog)**: the table from §0 is now the natural output. Each row has evidence (KQL count), AD state (PowerShell join), and a proposed action tied to an owner.

## 4. Dependency Qualification Matrix

An unqualified dependency is a rollback risk. The three dimensions below match the columns used in the §0 deliverable table — same vocabulary throughout.

| Criterion | Example values | Priority impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blast radius | Critical, Important, Standard | Critical goes first |
| RC4 exposure | Frequent, Occasional, Rare | Frequent goes first (thresholds tied to the §1 observation window) |
| Fix cost | Low, Medium, High | Low cost surfaces the quick wins |

Simple scoring model (low fix cost intentionally rewarded so easy fixes bubble up first):

- Blast radius: Critical=3, Important=2, Standard=1
- Exposure: Frequent=3, Occasional=2, Rare=1
- Fix cost: Low=3, Medium=2, High=1
- Total score = sum (max 9)

Interpretation:

- 8-9: remediate immediately
- 6-7: remediate in next wave
- 3-5: schedule after quick wins, or candidate for decommissioning

### Worked scoring example

Apply the model to the five dependencies surfaced in §3:

| Dependency | Blast radius | Exposure | Fix cost | **Score** | Wave |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `svc_sql_legacy` (prod DB) | Critical = 3 | Frequent = 3 | Medium = 2 (password rotation + app retest, 38 dependent clients) | **8** | Immediate |
| `krbtgt/PARTNER.LEGACY` (trust) | Critical = 3 | Frequent = 3 | Medium = 2 (cross-forest coord) | **8** | Immediate |
| `printsrv-bio-01$` (biomed printers) | Important = 2 | Frequent = 3 | High = 1 (vendor firmware) | **6** | Next wave + exception meanwhile |
| `svc_iis_intranet` (RC4 leak despite fresh AES key) | Important = 2 | Occasional = 2 | Medium = 2 (investigate client negotiation) | **6** | Next wave |
| `fileserver-old$` (legacy SMB share) | Standard = 1 | Rare = 1 | Medium = 2 | **4** | After quick wins / decommission candidate |

What to read from this table:

- The two 8-9 scores justify dedicated change windows and pre-stakeholder communication.
- `printsrv-bio-01$` is the typical "temporary exception" candidate: high exposure but you cannot fix it this quarter — document the exception with an expiry date and a vendor track.
- `fileserver-old$` at score 4 is often the cue to ask the harder question: should this service still exist?

## 5. Concrete RC4 Dependency Examples

Example A - Legacy service account (the stale-key trap, see Pattern D below):

- Signal: recurring RC4 in 4769 for `MSSQLSvc/app01`.
- AD state: `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes=0x1C` (AES+RC4 advertised).
- Runtime state: AES keys missing or stale because the account password predates AES enablement and was never rotated.
- Inventory action: tag as "quick win after secret rotation and app validation".

Example B - Third-party device:

- Signal: RC4-only requests from a non-Windows device IP.
- Observation: no AES negotiation from client side.
- Inventory action: tag as "temporary exception" with vendor remediation track.

Example C - Trust path:

- Signal: persistent RC4 patterns in cross-domain traffic.
- Observation: trust hardening not complete.
- Inventory action: tag as "trust wave" with cross-domain validation prerequisites.

### Detection signatures for each pattern

Each pattern above corresponds to a recognizable signature in the telemetry. The actual queries (KQL, PowerShell, SIEM saved searches) live in the **RC4 telemetry & inventory reporting** annex — the conceptual signatures below are what you ask the annex queries to surface.

**Pattern A — RC4 service ticket recurrence per SPN** (catches example A, the classic legacy service account). Signature: a stable daily count of `4769 + TicketEncryptionType == 0x17` for the same `ServiceName`, above a baseline threshold. Stable count = production traffic, not a one-off. Use a per-day binning over the observation window so you see the rhythm (business-hours peak, weekend drop) rather than a single number.

**Pattern B — RC4-only client** (catches example B, the third-party device or legacy appliance). Signature: a source IP that produces `4769` events where **every single ticket** is RC4 (`0x17`) and zero are AES (`0x11`/`0x12`) over the entire observation window. An IP that has *never once* negotiated AES is almost certainly a non-Windows / appliance / legacy stack with a hardcoded etype list. Each one is a vendor-track row in the backlog.

**Pattern C — RC4 on cross-domain referrals** (catches example C, the trust still using legacy crypto). Signature: `4769 + TicketEncryptionType == 0x17` where `ServiceName` starts with `krbtgt/<OTHER-REALM>` (anything other than your own realm). This is the smoking gun for a trust object that either has RC4 capability set or has a stale trust password preventing AES key derivation. Each distinct target realm = one trust to remediate.

Two further signatures worth surfacing during the inventory phase, even though they don't each map to a single example:

**Pattern D — AES-capable account still emitting RC4** (the stale-key trap). Signature: an account whose `msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes` is unset / `0x07` (default RC4-only inherited from `DefaultDomainSupportedEncTypes`) or `0x1C` (AES+RC4) that appears as the target of `4769 + 0x17` events. The AES capability is declared but the actual AES key was never derived because the password predates AES support and has never been rotated, so the KDC selects RC4 from the intersection of allowed etypes. *Note*: post-November 2022 (KB5020805), an account explicitly set to `0x18` (AES-only) cannot exhibit this pattern — if no AES key is present the KDC returns `KRB5KDC_ERR_ETYPE_NOSUPP` instead of falling back to RC4.

**Pattern E — Kdcsvc events on patched DCs (KB5073381).** Signature: events 201-209 from provider `Microsoft-Windows-Kerberos-Key-Distribution-Center` in the System log on any DC running Phase 1 or Phase 2 (`RC4DefaultDisablementPhase ≥ 1`). The KDC itself does the classification work: 201/206 identify the client (collapses into Pattern B), 202/207 identify the service with no AES key (collapses into Pattern D), 205 is a DC-side hygiene finding (fix on the DC, not in the inventory). Article 1 §6 contains the full grid. Treat Kdcsvc as the *first cut* of the inventory whenever it is available — it eliminates entire classes of correlation work. Always still cross-validate the top hits with 4768/4769 before scheduling remediation, because one Kdcsvc event covers a single negotiation, not the volumetric picture.

## 7. Expected Inventory Outputs

Minimum deliverables:

- A "dependency -> evidence -> criticality -> action" matrix.
- A quick-win list (high impact, low effort).
- A complex-case list with identified technical owner.
- A dated and justified temporary exception list.

Recommended backlog format:

| Dependency | Evidence | Risk | Action | Owner | Due date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `svc_sql_legacy` | Frequent RC4 in 4769 | High | Rotate secret + re-test | DB Team | W+1 |
| `device-biomed-01` | RC4-only advertisement | High | Temporary exception + vendor plan | Infra Team | W+2 |

## 8. Inventory Definition of Done

Inventory is "ready" only if:

- Top RC4 dependencies are identified and ranked.
- Each dependency has linked technical evidence (Kdcsvc 201-209 entry, 4768/4769 record, or AD attribute snapshot).
- Each item has an owner and target action.
- Exceptions are explicit, bounded, and tracked.
- The current `RC4DefaultDisablementPhase` value is documented for **every** DC, and Kdcsvc 201-209 has been baselined per DC (Article 1 §6). DCs still on Phase 0 with KB5073381 deployed are themselves an inventory finding — the registry valve must be turned to `1` before this source produces any signal.

Without these five conditions, remediation will start with hidden risk.
